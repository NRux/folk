The Star That Wins the Year

In the high country near Ruapehu, the dawn light breaks over the eastern horizon, revealing a single, brilliant point of light that outshines the faint cluster of stars nearby. For Ngāti Rangi and nei

Rowan Pike · October 8, 2026 · 6 min read

In this storySources & further readingAll stories

Across Aotearoa, the Māori name for New Zealand, the new year is approached through a sky that differs from one place to another. Maunga, or mountains, can shape what is visible above a community’s horizon. For some iwi, kinship communities connected through shared ancestry and place, Puanga is the seasonal anchor. Western astronomy names this star Rigel, the brightest in the constellation Orion. Matariki is the Māori name for the star cluster also called the Pleiades, and the new-year observance associated with its rising. Te Papa describes Puanga traditions in parts of the Far North and South Island, as well as Taranaki and Whanganui. Te Tai Tokerau names the northern North Island; Taranaki lies on the island’s western coast, while Whanganui names both a river and the communities connected with it. In Whanganui river valleys, the terrain can obscure Matariki while leaving Puanga visible. The distinction is local, not a fixed rule that one star always rises a week before the other.[7][8][9]

This regional variation is not a rejection of Matariki, but a practical and spiritual adaptation to the landscape. In a University of Auckland article, Te Kahuratai Moko-Painting draws on knowledge handed down by tohunga, expert holders of knowledge, such as Rereata Makiha. His account explains why some iwi in Aotearoa mark the New Year with Puanga, while Matariki has a different place in the sky and in regional traditions [1].

A Shared Whakapapa of Stars

The distinction between Puanga and Matariki is framed within a shared whakapapa, or genealogy, of stars. Moko-Painting describes the two as sisters, with Puanga as the older sister and Matariki as the younger, beautiful one. A traditional whakataukī, a proverbial saying, encapsulates this dynamic: “Ko Puanga, ko tōna mahi he toa-whawhai ki ā Matariki; te take, he tohe kia riro te tau i ā ia.” This translates to Puanga’s role being to spar with Matariki to become the star of the year.

This narrative underscores that the choice of star is rooted in local visibility and ancestral guidance rather than a single, monolithic calendar. Professor Rangi Matamua, identified in the 2025 account as the government’s advisor on knowledge of Matariki, notes that while the specific stars marking the beginning of the Māori new year may vary by region, the underlying themes of the ceremony remain consistent. Both Puanga and Matariki are celebrations of an ancient lunar-solar timekeeping system. The 2025 theme invited people to learn about the Māori New Year together while acknowledging communities that observe Puanga.

The Rhythms of Maramataka

Beyond the specific stars, the Māori New Year is embedded within the maramataka, the Māori lunar calendar. Ayla Hoeta, a maramataka practitioner, lecturer, and first-year doctoral candidate in the Faculty of Engineering and Design at the University of Auckland, describes maramataka as a way of understanding the sacredness of time. It is not merely a calendar but a system that connects people with the rhythms of the moon, stars, tides, and the body.

Hoeta describes maramataka as a way of paying attention to time in everyday life. For instance, the phase of Whiro, the dark phase marking the beginning of a new moon cycle, is associated with rangahau (research), wānanga (meaningful discussions), grief, and reflection. This distinction is crucial: Whiro is often associated with darkness, but this should not be mistaken for negativity. It is a time for inward focus and preparation.

The holiday falls on a different date each year, with its timing set close to the rising of Matariki and the Tangaroa phase, a named period in the maramataka’s lunar cycle. Hoeta notes that Matariki is a key tohu (sign) of the maramataka, gaining recognition as the main celebratory event embedded in policy. However, she argues that the holiday offers an opportunity to look at the wider system in which the celebration sits, inviting people to look around and within, not just up.

Regional Practices and Food

The observance of Puanga is deeply connected to local food systems and seasonal abundance. The whakataukī “Puanga kai rau” highlights the abundant harvest associated with this time. This connection is vividly illustrated by the kererū (New Zealand Pigeon). During the Puanga season, kererū are fat after eating berries of miro and tawa, native trees. The large amount of berries ferments in the bird’s stomach, causing drunkenness and making the birds easy to catch. This made kererū the food of choice during Puanga celebrations, with the first bird traditionally given to the senior women of the whānau, the extended family.

Puanga celebrations are not confined to a single day. Instead, they mark a period of approximately a month or longer, involving at least two months of preparation followed by two months of wānanga. The first new moon in the month of Pipiri, a winter month in the Māori calendar is when stars like Puanga, Matariki, and Whānui set, providing a chance to reflect on the past year and remember loved ones. Two weeks later, these stars rise again in the eastern sky, a time to acknowledge the rising of loved ones who have passed and to prepare for the new year.

The Ceremony of Whāngai

The Whāngai i te Hautapu ceremony is a central practice for acknowledging the rising of Matariki. It involves offering food to the stars by releasing steam from a cooked meal into the sky. Before the rising of Matariki, special food is taken from the gardens, forests, rivers, and ocean, and cooked in an earth oven or other cooking method. The steam rising into the sky feeds Matariki, symbolizing the connection between the earth and the celestial realm.

Te Papa's home ceremony guide suggests choosing food from several domains: land, sky, freshwater, and ocean. Its examples connect these with Tipuānuku, Tipuārangi, Waitī, and Waitā, stars associated respectively with food from the land, sky, freshwater, and ocean; they are examples rather than an exhaustive account of the cluster.

Te Papa's guide asks participants to gather before sunrise. After the karakia, a prayer or invocation, for Pōhutukawa, the star associated with remembering the dead, and the calling of names, the pot is opened for its steam offering; the remaining karakia follow. Its shorter home version is adapted from a larger ceremony.

Living Knowledge and Community

The revitalization of Matariki and Puanga traditions is a living process, involving both academic research and community practice. Ayla Hoeta helped design a playground beside the Puhinui stream in Manukau, in the Auckland area. She describes it as a way to bring maramataka knowledge into children's play and help restore the place.

Hoeta’s research also extends globally, including a project in Antarctica exploring how mātauranga Māori, Māori knowledge, could sit alongside Antarctic science and climate research. She has met Indigenous women revitalizing their lunar knowledge in Hawai‘i, Ra’iātea, and Canada, noting a common theme: the need for practical tools that help Indigenous communities understand ancestral knowledge.

The growth in understanding of Matariki is evident in public attitudes. Professor Matamua notes that 75% of New Zealanders see Matariki as a chance to celebrate the culture, people, and stories of Aotearoa, up from 70% in 2023. This trend suggests a deepening appreciation for Matariki across the country. International institutions, including NASA and the Royal Observatory in Greenwich, are also keen to engage with and grow their understanding of knowledge of Matariki and Puanga.

As communities continue to engage in the revitalization of these traditions, there is an opportunity to honor regional variations of the maramataka and acknowledge the stars that have guided different iwi in different ways. Whether celebrating Puanga or Matariki, the goal is to honor the shared whakapapa and the living knowledge that connects people to the whenua, the land, and the stars.

Looking from a particular place

A sky photograph makes one object visible, but a regional observance is not reducible to that image. The distinction between Matariki and Puanga is also a distinction about where people look and which knowledge they use. Te Papa's explanation keeps those regional differences central.[2] Images of the Pleiades and Rigel can help identify the celestial objects, while the meaning of an observance remains with the communities practicing it.

That distinction matters when sharing the tradition with a wider audience. A useful introduction can name common themes without claiming that every iwi follows one ceremony or reads the same signs. Specificity is a form of respect: it leaves room for a local account to differ from a national summary. The reader's task is to keep asking whose practice is being described, rather than allowing one recognizable star image to answer every question.
