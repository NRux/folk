The Living Soil of Xochimilco

The water in the canals of Xochimilco does not just reflect the sky; it supports life. Here, in the southern basin of Mexico City, agriculture happens on floating artificial islands known as chinampas

Rowan Pike · October 8, 2026 · 7 min read

In this storySources & further readingAll stories

The water in the canals of Xochimilco does not just reflect the sky; it supports life. Here, in the southern basin of Mexico City, agriculture happens on floating artificial islands known as chinampas. These are not static plots of land but dynamic ecosystems built from sediments, branches, and decaying vegetation. For over a millennium, this system has provided food and ecological stability to the region. Yet today, the survival of this heritage faces a complex set of pressures: soil degradation, generational gaps, and the economic challenges of competing with industrial agriculture. To understand how Xochimilco continues to function, we must look beyond the romantic image of the trajinera ride and examine the physical, social, and economic mechanisms that keep these islands alive.

A Heritage of Resilience

The chinampa system is an articulated network of wetland raised fields. The Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) designated it a Globally Important Agricultural Heritage System (GIAHS) in 2017, recognizing its historical depth and ecological sophistication. This is not merely a historical vestige. It is an active agricultural system that has adapted to hydrologic constraints and the high food demand of a major metropolis.

The productivity of the chinampas relies on biological stability. Farmers use sophisticated methods, including multiple cropping and crop shifts, to maintain yield without depleting the soil. The system supports a remarkable diversity of life. Local producers manage 51 domesticated species, while the surrounding environment hosts 96 non-domesticated species. These wild plants serve critical functions: 67% are used as fodder, 20% for medicinal purposes, and 13% for food. This biodiversity extends to the water itself. The canals and small ponds support aquatic communities that provide habitat for birds and small animals. The chinampa is, therefore, both a farm and a wetland, a dual identity that defines its ecological value.

Healing the Soil

Despite its historical resilience, the soil in Xochimilco faces specific threats. Between 2000 and 2019, Mexico accumulated 72 percent of land degradation, a statistic that underscores the urgency of soil health initiatives. In Xochimilco, salinity is identified as a primary problem affecting productivity. This is where the Global Soil Doctor Program (PDS) enters the picture. Promoted nationally by the UNAM’s Programa Universitario de Estudios Interdisciplinarios del Suelo (PUEIS) since 2021, the program trains farmers to diagnose the health of their own land.

The training is practical and low-cost. Participants learn to excavate a one-meter square pit to analyze soil horizons, texture, pH, and organic matter. It is described as a clinical chart for the earth. Lourdes Fuentes Martínez and Macrina Dehesa Sánchez, two certified "Soil Doctors," illustrate the impact of this knowledge. Macri, who inherited her plot from her father, notes that when she started, the land was completely infertile. After applying the diagnostic techniques and sustainable management practices, she reports that seeds now sprout spontaneously. For Lourdes, the soil training also opened a way to share what she learned with others. Together with 681 others, these farmers have been certified in 16 states, creating a network of local experts capable of maintaining the biological integrity of the chinampas.

The Axolotl as a Metric of Health

The health of the chinampa is inextricably linked to the health of the water. The axolotl (*Ambystoma mexicanum*) serves as a biological indicator for this ecosystem. Linked to Mexican culture for over 1,500 years, the axolotl requires clean, oxygenated water to survive. The Adoptaxolotl campaign, led by the UNAM’s Laboratory of Ecological Restoration, uses the preservation of this species as a central goal. The university's account describes a colony of approximately 120 axolotls and efforts to improve canal conditions ahead of a possible return to the wild.

This is not just a conservation effort; it is an economic and social intervention. The campaign aims to provide financial support to chinamperos so they can continue their traditional work and rehabilitate secondary canals. Currently, 21 chinamperos receive financing and accompaniment from this project. Luis Zambrano González, a researcher at the Institute of Biology, argues that the survival of the axolotl depends on the entire community, not just the farmers. He calls for a collective responsibility: consuming local produce, supporting the campaign, and respecting the site as a fundamental space for agricultural production and urban resilience. The "chinampa refugio" initiative proposes establishing specific plots that are free of agrochemicals, serving as safe havens for the amphibians. This approach links ecological restoration directly to the livelihoods of the growers, creating a feedback loop where economic support enables environmental care.

Bridging the Urban-Rural Divide

For the chinampa system to remain economically viable, it must connect with the urban market. Arca Tierra, a project based in Xochimilco, has developed a model that bridges this gap. Led by Lucio Usobiaga, the organization connects rural producers with urban consumers through regenerative agriculture and fair trade. They offer over 200 varieties of food, including vegetables, flowers, and native maize products.

Arca Tierra’s approach is distinct because it values the knowledge of the farmer as the primary asset. They use "chapines," which are described as "micro versions of the chinampa," to germinate seeds manually. This technique avoids the use of plastic trays and synthetic substrates, aligning with the principles of the ancestral system. By selling directly to households and restaurants in Mexico City, Arca Tierra creates a market for high-quality, locally produced food. This economic linkage is crucial. As Zambrano notes, the chinamperos must survive the "hecatombe" caused by modern industrial agriculture. Providing a premium market for their produce offers a pathway to economic stability that does not rely on state subsidies alone.

Reclaiming Space and Identity

The social fabric of Xochimilco is also undergoing transformation. The Escuela Campesina, launched in March 2023 by the Iniciativa Agroecológica Xochimilco (IAX) and Arca Tierra, aims to train the next generation of farmers. The first generation consisted of five students, four of whom were women. This demographic shift is significant. Traditionally, Xochimilco held a belief that women should not work the chinampa soil. The land was personified as a jealous woman, and it was believed that if a woman touched the soil, it would become salty and the crop would fail.

Virginia "Vero" Martínez Guevara, a 47-year-old producer and student, recounts how this norm was a barrier to her participation. She faced harassment and exclusion for years. "It was very difficult," she recalls. "Phrases like 'you are old, what are you doing here' were bread and butter." The Escuela Campesina has challenged these norms. By providing a structured environment for learning agroecological techniques and preserving chinampa culture, the program has created a space where women can develop their skills and be valued. Vero notes that she now feels safe and appreciated, part of a team that shares knowledge. This social restructuring is essential for the future of the system. If the knowledge is not passed to the next generation, and if half the population is excluded from the labor force, the chinampa system will fade.

Conclusion

The chinampas of Xochimilco are not a museum. They are a living system that requires active management, economic support, and social inclusion. The designation as a GIAHS recognizes its past, but its future depends on present-day actions. The soil doctors are healing the land. The axolotl campaign is cleaning the water and supporting the economy. Arca Tierra is building the market. The Escuela Campesina is building the community. Together, these efforts address the physical, social, and economic conditions necessary for a distinctive culture to continue in a place. The challenge is to maintain this balance in a rapidly urbanizing city, ensuring that the chinampas remain a source of food, identity, and ecological resilience for Mexico City.
