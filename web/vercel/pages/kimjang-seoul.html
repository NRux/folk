The Hands That Remember: Kimjang in a Changing Seoul

The Weight of the Red Paste

What does it feel like to have a recipe in your hands that is also a history lesson? For many in Seoul, South Korea’s capital, the answer comes with the smell of salt, garlic, and crushed red pepper. It is a scent that signals the arrival of late autumn, a time when the air turns crisp and the communal work of kimjang begins: making and sharing kimchi, vegetables preserved through seasoning and fermentation. This is not merely about preparing food for the winter; it is an act of cultural preservation, a way of carrying family knowledge across generations while adapting to a world that is increasingly fragmented.

Kimjang, the practice of making and sharing kimchi, was inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2013. The designation recognizes more than just a dish. It acknowledges a social ritual that reaffirms identity and strengthens family cooperation. Kimchi itself is defined as preserved vegetables seasoned with spices and fermented seafood, forming an essential part of Korean meals that transcends class and regional differences. Yet, as we look at how this tradition is practiced today, we see a tension between the deep roots of this custom and the modern pressures that are reshaping how food is produced, shared, and consumed.

The Yearly Rhythm of Preparation

The preparation of kimchi is not a spontaneous decision made in a heat of hunger. It follows a strict yearly cycle that ties the household to the seasons. The preparations begin well before the autumn gathering. Seafood is preserved, salt is obtained, and peppers are dried at different points in the year; kimjang draws these separate tasks together.

Late autumn is kimjang season. This is when communities collectively make and share large quantities of kimchi to ensure that every household has enough to sustain it through the long, harsh winter. Weather matters to the timing of the work, a practical detail that links the household's plans to the season. A recipe can also carry a family's accumulated knowledge, handed from one generation to another. This transmission is not just about passing down a list of ingredients; it is about handing over a set of skills, a sense of timing, and a role within the family structure.

Hands-On Tradition and Family Memory

The physical act of making kimchi is labor-intensive and deeply personal. Traditional techniques are precise and require a specific state of mind. One Korean Food Master, Yu Jeongim, recalls learning these skills from her mother, who operated a side dish shop in the market. Yu Jeongim’s mother taught her to handle kimchi with completely dry hands and to press down on the spot from which kimchi was taken to block contact with the air. These small, seemingly minor details are part of a larger body of knowledge that ensures the quality and longevity of the food.

Yu Jeongim also learned that the saeujeot, or salted shrimp, used for kimchi has to be yukjeot, which is fermented shrimp caught in the sixth lunar month, for the kimchi to taste good. She was taught to use glutinous rice water for cabbage kimchi and flour water for young summer radish kimchi. These distinctions show that kimjang is not a monolithic practice. It is a living tradition that varies by family, by region, and by the specific ingredients available. The knowledge is passed down through observation and participation, often in the context of daily life rather than formal instruction. Yu Jeongim’s grandmother was famous in her village for her cooking skills and was usually called upon for neighborhood feasts. This history of communal cooking highlights how kimjang has always been a social event, a way for neighbors to come together and share the burden of preparation.

Community Scales and Modern Festivals

In recent years, the scale of kimjang has expanded beyond the family unit to include large community events. These festivals serve as a way to preserve the tradition while also addressing social needs. The 2019 Seoul Kimchi Festival was held from November 1 to 3 at Seoul Plaza and the Mugyo-ro area. Under the theme of “sharing and regional mutual prosperity,” the event provided 3,500 citizens with a chance to make and share kimchi. Over two days, these participants made 65 tons of kimchi. This massive undertaking demonstrates the power of collective labor. It transforms a domestic task into a public spectacle, one that highlights the value of cooperation and sharing.

The festival also included a “Kimchi Museum” that showed all about kimjang, and a “Kimchi Market” selling kimchi and its ingredients. There were cooking shows, children’s classes, and even a campaign to support pork farms facing difficulties due to African Swine Fever outbreaks. This integration of food production with economic support and education shows how kimjang is being reinvented to fit contemporary social and economic contexts. It is no longer just about feeding a family; it is about building community, supporting local agriculture, and promoting cultural heritage.

Another example is the Garak Kimjang Festival, which took place on November 17, 2025, at Garak Market in Songpa-gu, a district of Seoul. This event is celebrating its 18th anniversary, having been a continuous tradition of giving since 2008. In 2025, participants collectively made an estimated 80,000 heads of kimchi. The entirety of the finished product was slated for delivery to 10,000 vulnerable households across Seoul. This event highlights the charitable dimension of kimjang. It is a way for the community to care for those who are most in need, using the traditional practice of making kimchi as a vehicle for generosity. The labor relies on a diverse group of volunteers, including local citizens, foreign residents, and children. This inclusivity shows that kimjang is a practice that can transcend social boundaries, bringing together people from different backgrounds to work toward a common goal.

The Shrinking Scale and Changing Roles

Despite the vibrancy of these festivals, the traditional scale of kimjang has begun to shrink. Factors such as changing family structures, reduced domestic kimchi consumption, and a general labor shortage have led to a decrease in the overall volume of homemade kimchi. The sight of entire neighborhoods coordinating this effort is now rare. This change reflects broader social shifts in South Korea. Families are smaller, work schedules are longer, and the convenience of store-bought kimchi is increasingly appealing. The labor-intensive nature of traditional kimjang, which can span several days and require the hard work of entire families, is becoming less feasible for many households.

This shift raises questions about the future of the tradition. If the communal aspect of kimjang is lost, what remains? Is it just a recipe? Or is it a cultural identity? The fact that kimjang is still practiced, even if on a smaller scale, suggests that it holds a deep emotional and cultural significance for many Koreans. It is a way of connecting to the past, of honoring the labor of previous generations, and of maintaining a sense of continuity in a rapidly changing world.

Preservation and Adaptation

The preservation of kimjang is not just about keeping the recipes alive. It is about maintaining the social structures and values that underpin the practice. The transmission of knowledge from mother-in-law to daughter-in-law, the communal labor of festivals, and the charitable giving of events like the Garak Kimjang Festival all contribute to this preservation. However, this preservation is not static. It is an ongoing process of adaptation and reinvention.

Museum Kimchikan, established in 1986 in Seoul and acquired by the Korean food company Pulmuone in 1987, plays a role in this process. Named one of the 11 world’s top food museums by CNN, it offers experience programs where visitors can make kimchi for themselves and learn the history and culture of kimchi first-hand. This institutionalization of kimjang helps to ensure that the knowledge is not lost, even as the traditional domestic practice evolves. It provides a space for learning and engagement, allowing people to connect with the tradition in a structured and educational setting.

The challenge for the future is to balance preservation with adaptation. How can the tradition be kept alive in a world where time is scarce and convenience is paramount? The answer may lie in the very nature of kimjang itself. It is a practice that is inherently flexible, capable of being scaled up or down, and of being adapted to different contexts. Whether it is a family making a few jars in their kitchen or a community making tens of thousands of heads for distribution, the core values of sharing, cooperation, and respect for nature remain the same.

Unresolved Tensions

There is an unresolved tension in the current state of kimjang. On one hand, there is a strong desire to preserve the tradition and its cultural significance. On the other hand, there are practical and economic pressures that are making it difficult to maintain the traditional scale of production. The labor involved is significant, and the time required is substantial. For many modern households, this is a burden that is hard to bear.

This tension is not unique to kimjang. It is a reflection of a broader shift in how food is produced and consumed. The move from local, home-produced food to global, industrially-produced food is a trend that is seen in many cultures. Kimjang is a counter-trend, a way of asserting the value of local, communal, and hand-made food. But it is a counter-trend that is facing increasing resistance from the forces of modernity.

The future of kimjang will depend on how well it can adapt to these changing conditions. Will it continue to be a practice that is rooted in the home and the community? Or will it become a commercialized product, sold in supermarkets and restaurants? The evidence suggests that it is already moving in both directions. The existence of festivals and museums shows that there is a strong desire to preserve the cultural aspect of kimjang. But the growth of the commercial kimchi industry shows that there is also a strong demand for convenience and consistency.
