The Stirring Pot: How Sumanak Anchors Belonging in Dushanbe

The scent of roasted wheat and butter is a specific kind of memory. In Dushanbe, the capital of Tajikistan in Central Asia, it signals the arrival of Navruz, the local name for the spring new year also known as Nowruz, but for many households, it marks something more precise: the moment the family gathers around a large cauldron. The dish at the center of this gathering is sumanak, a thick, sweet paste made from sprouted wheat, flour, and oil. It is not merely food; it is a communal act that requires patience, coordination, and shared labor. As the capital prepares for its public celebrations, the private ritual of making sumanak offers a clear window into how tradition is taught, maintained, and adapted across generations.

A Communal Rhythm

The preparation of sumanak is distinctively collaborative. It is rarely a solitary kitchen task. In many Tajik households, the work is divided by role and energy. Women often take the lead in the continuous stirring, a task that requires constant attention to prevent the mixture from burning or clumping. This labor is accompanied by song and movement, turning the cooking process into a performance. Men frequently assist by managing the fire, carrying heavy cauldrons, and preparing the raw ingredients. This division of labor creates a shared space where different roles converge around a single goal.

The UNESCO inscription of the "Culture of Sumanak/Sumalak cooking" in 2025 highlights this communal aspect as a key element of the practice. UNESCO places the shared work of preparing sumanak alongside its seasonal meaning. The gathering around the pot gives neighbors and relatives time to renew ties. It is a practice that makes belonging tangible through shared effort. The pot itself becomes a focal point, a physical object around which relationships are negotiated and renewed.

The Long Wait

Before the boiling begins, there is a period of quiet anticipation. The wheat must be soaked, sprouted, and cared for over a period of two to three weeks. This stage is often the most personal and intimate part of the process. In many families, a single person is designated to tend to the sprouting wheat, watering it daily and ensuring it grows in the dark. This caretaking role is not just about agriculture; it is a ritual of patience.

Shakho Ruzimatova, a resident of Dushanbe, recalls her childhood experiences with this practice. She remembers her grandmother and mother preparing sumanak, and how she participated by observing and assisting. For her, the most cherished aspect was not the cooking itself, but the gathering. Relatives and neighbors would come together, children would play games, and women would sing while tending the fire. This memory of togetherness is what drives her to continue the tradition in her own family. The act of tending the wheat, even in its quiet, pre-cooking phase, serves as a bridge between the past and the present, connecting the cook to the generations that came before.

Professionalizing the Tradition

While sumanak is deeply rooted in domestic life, it has also entered the sphere of professional craft. In Khusoniyon, a district of Tajikistan, 63-year-old Mavluda Sotivoldieva has been preparing sumanak professionally for six years. She uses a 300-liter cauldron, producing enough for both her family and for sale. Her journey illustrates how traditional knowledge can be scaled and sustained. She learned the craft from her mother, a common path for many practitioners, and she has, in turn, taught her granddaughters and daughters-in-law.

Mavluda’s work highlights the physical demands of the process. The preparation takes approximately one day of continuous effort, a time commitment that deters many from attempting it. She describes the craft as an art that requires dedication and strength. By selling sumanak, she not only preserves the tradition but also creates an economic livelihood around it. Her son and daughter-in-law help by transporting the finished product to the market in the city of Bokhtar, where it is sold for 20 somoni, in Tajikistan’s currency, per liter. This commercial aspect does not diminish the cultural value; rather, it demonstrates the versatility of the practice. It can be a private family ritual, a public community event, or a professional service.

Teaching and Transmission

People learn to make sumanak in several settings. Family practice is one route; schools and cultural gatherings offer others. This intergenerational transfer ensures that the nuances of the craft are not lost. The specific techniques, such as the precise timing of stirring or the care taken in sprouting the wheat, are learned through observation and participation.

In Dushanbe, this transmission is supported by public initiatives. The Navruz Caravan, a public event that brings the celebration of spring to the capital, serves as a platform for showcasing these traditions. It allows residents and visitors to witness the practices that are usually confined to private homes. By bringing the preparation of sumanak into the public eye, these events validate the cultural significance of the dish and encourage wider participation. The knowledge is also shared through schools and cultural events, ensuring that younger generations are exposed to the practice even if they do not live in households where it is regularly prepared.

Continuing Possibilities

The practice of making sumanak continues to evolve while maintaining its core identity. It is a tradition that is both ancient and adaptable. The use of modern tools, such as meat grinders for crushing the sprouted wheat, has streamlined some aspects of the process, but the essential elements of communal labor, patience, and shared enjoyment remain unchanged. The dish continues to serve as a symbol of unity, prosperity, and renewal, connecting people across age and social boundaries.

As Dushanbe prepares for Navruz, the sumanak pot remains a central symbol of the celebration. It is a tangible object that holds the weight of history and the promise of the future. Through the shared act of stirring, singing, and eating, the tradition makes belonging tangible. It is a practice that does not just mark the change of seasons; it reinforces the social fabric that sustains the community. In a rapidly changing world, the sumanak pot offers a steady anchor, a reminder that some forms of connection are built through patience, care, and the simple act of sharing a meal.

The work around a shared pot

A shared preparation gathers more than ingredients. People coordinate time, divide tasks, make room for one another, and recognize what needs to happen next. That organization can be easy to overlook when the finished dish becomes the main image of a celebration. Paying attention to the work around the pot makes the social dimension more concrete. Belonging appears in what people do together, not only in what they say the occasion represents.

The account of sumanak in Tajikistan belongs within that specific context. Nowruz is observed across different countries and communities, and an international description does not make every preparation identical.[1] Images from another country can show a related dish only when their location is named. They should not be captioned as a scene in Dushanbe simply because a viewer might recognize the shared celebration.

For a reader encountering the practice from outside, the most useful questions concern participation. Who carries the preparation knowledge? How is someone new brought into the work? Which parts of the occasion allow older and younger people to spend time together? These questions make seasonal renewal easier to understand as an activity sustained by relationships. They also avoid turning a researched cultural essay into an instruction manual or claiming that one described gathering represents everyone.
