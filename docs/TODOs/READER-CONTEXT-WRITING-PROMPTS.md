# Reader context: drafting, revision and review prompts

Image selection direction: prioritize group shots and show a creative practice's
effects on people, including collective work, audiences, dancing and shared
venues. Avoid close-up portraits and repetitive subjects. For music, use at most
one equipment image, then vary social scenes. Prefer verified period photographs;
otherwise retain the actual place/date. Inspect every image visually and match
the caption to what is visible, not merely its filename. Keep the one-image-per-
two-paragraph rule and all source/creator/license credits. Follow
`../platform/EDITORIAL-STYLE.md` for the full policy.

Apply alongside the existing persona brief, source/claim requirements, article
length and image rules in `../platform/EDITORIAL-STYLE.md`. Preserve each persona's
voice. Explain unfamiliar English vocabulary as well as non-English terms.

## Drafting prompt

Write for a curious reader who may know little about the place or practice.
On first use, give an unfamiliar term a concise, source-supported meaning and
its relevant cultural or geographic setting. Retain local words and diacritics.
Introduce people and places by their relationships to the story, not merely by
listing names. Let context deepen the prose: explain how a practice is learned,
how a community is organized, or how landscape shapes an observation where the
sources support that connection. Use apposition, nearby explanation or narrative
movement rather than repeated dictionary brackets. Keep the language lyrical
when appropriate without inventing symbolism, etymologies, beliefs, dialogue or
firsthand experience. Local names and astronomical identifiers can coexist;
neither naming system should be presented as the only legitimate one.

## Revision prompt

Read the draft as someone encountering its vocabulary for the first time.
Identify unfamiliar words and unexplained place/community names. Add enough
context at first use to understand each term's role, with source IDs for added
factual claims. Break up clusters of new terms across sentences or paragraphs.
Keep subsequent uses natural and avoid repetitive translations. Preserve the
writer's cadence, substantive argument and distinctions between documented
history and interpretation. If a cultural meaning is unsupported, flag it for
research rather than supplying a plausible story. Do not update private versions,
claim ledgers or publication state without the normal review/version process.

## Review prompt

Check whether first-use meaning and orientation are clear, precise and sourced.
Check that a place name has a location, a community name has an appropriate
explanation, a technical name has a function, and an astronomical name has an
observable referent. Check that explanation does not flatten a complex concept
into a misleading one-word translation or imply a universal belief. Return
specific missing-context locations and suggested edits with their source IDs.
Hold unsupported explanations for research. A fluency check does not replace
factual, rights, authorization or publication acceptance gates.

## Matariki/Puanga teaching example

Possible first-use introductions, chosen for the particular meaning in this story:

| Term | Reader orientation |
|---|---|
| Aotearoa | Aotearoa New Zealand; no unsupported name-origin story is needed. |
| maunga | Mountains; add a particular mountain's significance only with local evidence. |
| iwi | Māori kinship communities associated with ancestry and particular territories. |
| Matariki | The Pleiades star cluster, whose seasonal appearance marks the new year for many Māori communities; the word can also name the celebration. |
| Puanga | A star recognized by some iwi as a new-year marker, also called Rigel in astronomy; it is not part of the Matariki cluster. |
| Rigel | The astronomical name for that same star in Orion, rather than another new term without a referent. |
| Te Tai Tokerau | Orient the reader to the northern North Island and the specific locality discussed. |
| Taranaki | A region on the western North Island; distinguish the region from Taranaki Maunga where needed. |
| Whanganui | Identify the Whanganui River valley or the particular community meant, rather than leaving city, river and community interchangeable. |

Illustrative prose, not an automatic revision of the published story:

“In Aotearoa New Zealand, the new year is read against different horizons. Some
iwi, Māori kinship communities connected by ancestry and place, look for Puanga,
the star astronomers call Rigel in Orion. Others give prominence to Matariki,
the Pleiades star cluster whose return to the dawn sky marks the season. In the
Whanganui River valley, maunga, or mountains, can conceal the view toward
Matariki while leaving Puanga more visible. The distinction begins with where
people stand and what their horizon allows them to see.”

The last sentence expresses the passage's editorial connection to visibility;
it does not assert a universal explanation for all iwi. Introduce Te Tai Tokerau
and Taranaki when the narrative reaches those places, rather than forcing every
name into this introductory paragraph. Do not carry forward the original
blanket “one week before” timing or an unnecessary brightness ranking without
separate evidence; visibility and rising relationships vary with locality.
