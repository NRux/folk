The perspective behind the journal

Identity is something

we keep making.

Folkly begins with a question: how do people and places become distinct, and how does that distinctiveness change when the world around them changes?

Our editorial perspective grows out of Noah Rappaport’s Creative Cities project and his framework,

Adaptive Identity Through Struggle

, developed from the earlier

Cycles of Cultural Identity and Differentiation

. It proposes that recurring experiences of struggle, recovery, and creative response help shape the identities people inherit and make their own.

The adaptive identity cycle

The framework follows five connected moments. They are prompts for inquiry rather than a fixed sequence every community must follow.

01

Encounter & disruption

A community encounters change: new people, technologies, economic pressures, political conflict, or an altered environment. Existing ways of living meet a new condition.

02

Identity negotiation

People reconsider what matters, what belongs, and what they want to preserve. They may adopt outside influences, combine them with inherited practices, or resist them to protect a distinction.

03

Creative response

Responses take form in everyday problem-solving, music, food, craft, ritual, design, protest, and experimentation. Creativity gives people ways to meet needs and express what a change means.

04

Integration

Some responses become part of ordinary life. A new practice is repeated, taught, supported, and passed on. Spaces and institutions can help it take root.

05

Renewed identity

A community carries forward a changed understanding of itself. What began as a response can become an inheritance, available to the next generation when new challenges appear.

What struggle contributes

The framework’s central proposition is that struggle is necessary to the growth of adaptive identity. It directs attention to the work of responding: finding resources, making meaning, negotiating differences, and rebuilding a sense of belonging.

This is Noah’s working theoretical position, not an established universal law. The original model emphasized moderate, recoverable stress. Its application needs to account for the severity of a challenge, the support available, and the possibility of loss as well as growth. Celebrating a community’s response does not make the conditions it faced desirable.

Creativity across human needs

Drawing on Maslow as a starting lens, the framework connects creative activity to overlapping needs. Practical invention can help people secure everyday life. Craft, food, literature, and music can deepen belonging and cultural knowledge. Experimental art can open new questions about identity and possibility.

These are overlapping uses of creativity, not a ranking of cultures or art forms. A meal, a textile, or a song can meet practical, social, and expressive needs at the same time.

How this becomes a journal

Begin with the particular.

A place, a practice, a person, a material. Specific details keep a story grounded.

Ask what made it possible.

Look at relationships, resources, institutions, environments, and historical pressures.

Keep people in the story.

Communities make choices. Their work deserves more than a story in which hardship does all the explaining.

Let the story test the lens.

Different cases can challenge or refine the framework. The interpretation should remain open to evidence.

Folkly celebrates cultural life while asking deeper questions about its conditions. The framework offers a way into a story; the people, places, and evidence determine where the story goes.
